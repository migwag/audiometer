!

Not a medical diagnosis. This is a self-test for information only and does not replace an examination by an audiologist or doctor. It is made for music producers and listeners who want an EQ profile that compensates for their hearing loss. Results depend on your volume setting and calibration and on the frequency response of the headphones you use, so they can differ from a clinical audiogram by 10 dB or more. If you notice sudden hearing loss, a difference between your ears, ear pain or new tinnitus, see a professional.

How to read this, and its limits

  • Procedure. Each frequency starts at 40 dB HL, drops 10 dB after every response and rises 5 dB after every miss. Threshold is the lowest level heard on 2 ascending presentations. Order follows clinical practice: 1 kHz up to the highest frequency, a 1 kHz retest, then the low frequencies.
  • Reliability checks. Random silent catch trials and presses between tones count as false responses. A 1 kHz retest that differs by more than 10 dB means the run was inconsistent.
  • dB HL. 0 dB HL is the median threshold of young normal listeners at each frequency. Levels are converted with reference values for circumaural audiometric headphones (ISO 389-5 / 389-8). 18 and 20 kHz have no standard reference, so treat them as relative.
  • Calibration. Without a measured output level, absolute values can be off by 10 dB or more. Your headphones' own frequency response is folded into every threshold. The headphone correction removes your model's typical coloration up to about 10 kHz, but not unit-to-unit differences, fit, or the shape of your own ears. Without it, apply the EQ only on the same headphones at the same volume.
  • Air conduction only. No bone conduction and no masking. If one ear is 40 dB or more worse, sound can cross to the better ear and the worse ear will look better than it is.
  • Extended high frequencies drop with age even in healthy ears, so loss above 10 kHz is common and partly expected. It is often the earliest sign of noise damage, which makes it worth tracking over time.
  • Not a diagnosis. See an audiologist or ENT for sudden changes, a difference between ears, ear pain, or new tinnitus.
  • Using the EQ. Put it on the control-room or headphone output (monitor controller, Control Room / cue path, or a system-wide EQ), never on the mix bus. A mix balanced through it will sound dull to everyone else if it gets printed.
  • Do not chase the full loss. Large boosts above 10 kHz mostly add hiss and fatigue. Half-gain with an 18 dB ceiling is a sensible start; adjust by ear on familiar reference tracks.

Calibrating with an SPL meter

Back to the calibration field

The page needs one number: how loud a full-scale signal (0 dBFS) is at your headphones at your volume setting. You measure the 1 kHz reference tone, which plays 20 dB below full scale, and add 20. Every test tone is then set relative to that. You do not wear the headphones for this. You put the ear cup on a small flat coupler with the meter's microphone in it, which stands in for your ear.

  1. Set up the playback chain exactly as for the test

    Use the same headphones, cable, adapter, audio interface or output and sample rate you will test with. Turn off anything that changes level or tone: system EQ, spatial audio, loudness or volume normalization, noise cancelling, and any plugin on the output.

    Set the volume to the position you will test at and write it down, for example "interface headphone knob at 2 o'clock" or "macOS volume 10 of 16". The calibration is only valid for that setting.

  2. Build a flat-plate coupler

    Take a stiff board larger than the ear cup: MDF, thick cardboard or a cutting board. Make a hole just big enough for the microphone. Push the microphone through from below so its tip ends flush with the surface or up to 5 mm above it.

    Seal any gap around the microphone with tape or putty. Lay the board flat with the microphone pointing up.

  3. Set up the meter

    • Weighting: C or Z (flat). A-weighting also reads correctly at 1 kHz.
    • Response: Slow.
    • Range: one that covers 60–100 dB.
    • Calibrator: if you have an acoustic calibrator (94 dB at 1 kHz), check the meter with it first.
    • Measurement mic with REW or similar: load the mic's calibration file and calibrate the input level before you start.

  4. Check the background noise

    With nothing playing and a headphone cup on the coupler, read the meter. The tone reading in the next step should be at least 10 dB higher. If it isn't, find a quieter room or turn off fans and air conditioning.

  5. Place the cup and seal it

    Put one ear cup cushion-down on the board, centred over the microphone. Weigh it down gently, for example with the headband resting naturally or a paperback book on top, so the cushion seals all round without being crushed.

    Seal test: while the tone plays, press the cup a little harder. The reading should change by less than 1 dB. If it jumps, air is leaking past the cushion; reposition it or add weight.

  6. Measure both sides

    Start the reference tone below. It plays 1 kHz at −20 dBFS on both channels for 30 seconds. Wait 2–3 seconds for the reading to settle, then note it. Repeat for the other cup.

    Lift and reseat each cup once and measure again. Use the average of all readings. Repeated readings within 1 dB of each other are good.

  7. Enter the result and lock the volume

    Type your readings into the calculator. It adds 20 and fills in the calibration field. Do not touch the volume again until the test is done.

    Recalibrate whenever you change headphones, ear pads, cable, interface or output device, and every few months otherwise.

What readings to expect
  • 75–85 dB SPL is the ideal range. Enough headroom for loud test tones, and the quietest tones stay above the noise of your interface.
  • Above 90 dB: turn the volume down and measure again. The reference tone should never be uncomfortable.
  • Below 70 dB: turn the volume up, or the loudest test tones may be capped and show as "no response".
  • Left and right differ by more than 2 dB: check the seal first. If it stays, the headphones or output are unbalanced; correct it with the balance control or keep it in mind when reading the audiogram.
Accuracy and alternatives
  • A flat plate reads within roughly 3–5 dB of a real ear at 1 kHz. Clinics use a standardized artificial ear for this and are more exact.
  • This calibrates the level at 1 kHz only. Differences at other frequencies come from the headphones themselves; the headphone correction above handles most of that up to about 10 kHz.
  • Phone apps work only as a rough check. The phone's microphone can't be placed and sealed properly, so expect several dB of error. A small external measurement mic for the phone is much better.

Before you start

  1. Put the headphones on: red or "R" on the right ear. Remove glasses and earrings if they lift the cups.
  2. You will hear groups of short beeps, often very faint. Press Space (or tap the circle) as soon as you think you hear one, even if you are unsure.
  3. Don't try to predict the timing. Pauses between tones are random, and some trials are deliberately silent.
  4. Plan for 15–25 minutes. Press Esc to pause at any time.
